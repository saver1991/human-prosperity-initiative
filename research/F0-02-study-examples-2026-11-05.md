# F0-02 — Three verified study examples across research domains

**Date:** 5 November 2026 · **Status:** Targeted preliminary screening only, **not** a systematic review, representative sample, risk-of-bias assessment, meta-analysis, or intervention selection.

This note checks one peer-reviewed primary study in each initial domain. It deliberately includes a **challenging/negative** energy-efficiency result. Sources were located by targeted public web search and inspected through publisher/primary abstract records; see [dated query log](search-log-2026-11-05.csv). No full-text extraction, independent second screening or author contact has been performed.

## A. Access to support — SNAP benefits, United States (2019)

**Source:** Finkelstein, A., & Notowidigdo, M. J. (2019). [*Take-Up and Targeting: Experimental Evidence from SNAP*](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz013). *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 134(3), 1505–1556. [NBER abstract](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24652).

- **Design:** Randomised field experiment among approximately 30,000 older adults likely eligible for but not enrolled in SNAP.
- **Comparison:** Control (usual situation) vs information letter vs information **plus** help with applying.
- **Observed enrolment within nine months:** 6% control, 11% information-only and 18% information plus application assistance.
- **Primary takeaway for HPI:** Track **verified benefit receipt**, beyond awareness or applications. Assistance mattered in this context, but the paper also examines targeting and welfare implications; maximising registrations is **not** the same as optimising wellbeing.
- **Limits:** US institutional/eligibility context, specific age and enrolment population, eligibility uncertainty and resource intensity of assistance. We have **not** recalculated effect sizes, confidence intervals, intervention costs, risk of bias or social welfare.

## B. Essential costs — Michigan weatherization, United States (2018)

**Source:** Fowlie, M., Greenstone, M., & Wolfram, C. (2018). [*Do Energy Efficiency Investments Deliver? Evidence from the Weatherization Assistance Program*](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjy005). *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 133(3), 1597–1644. [NBER entry](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21331).

- **Design:** Experimental evaluation of a major residential weatherization programme covering roughly 30,000 Michigan households.
- **Finding reported in the journal abstract:** Up-front investment cost was about twice realised energy savings; modelled energy savings substantially exceeded actual savings. No statistically significant increase in indoor temperatures was found in the evaluated homes.
- **Primary takeaway for HPI:** Do not equate programme enrolment, engineering projections or policy counts with **realised affordability and wellbeing outcomes**. Cost-effectiveness can be unfavourable even for well-intended interventions.
- **Limits:** Specific US subsidy programme, vintage, local weather/buildings and evaluation design; does not demonstrate that **all** efficiency policies fail. Health, comfort, distributional and carbon effects require separate outcome analysis.

## C. Economic opportunity — Graduation programme in six countries (2015)

**Source:** Banerjee, A., Duflo, E., Goldberg, N., Karlan, D., Osei, R., Parienté, W., Shapiro, J., Thuysbaert, B., & Udry, C. (2015). [*A multifaceted program causes lasting progress for the very poor: Evidence from six countries*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1260799). *Science*, 348(6236), 1260799. [MIT open manuscript record](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/bd9702df-9dd2-4786-97bc-3b39fce6f081).

- **Design:** Six randomised trials with approximately 10,495 participants, across Ethiopia, Ghana, Honduras, India, Pakistan and Peru.
- **Intervention:** Productive asset transfer combined with training/coaching, temporary consumption support and selected savings/health components.
- **Findings as reported:** Higher earnings; household consumption improvement in five of six sites, with positive effects persisting at least one year after programme activities ended; estimated benefits exceeded programme costs in five sites.
- **Primary takeaway for HPI:** Durable gains are plausible but programmes can be intensive and expensive. Adaptation, local livelihoods and careful cost measurement are essential.
- **Limits:** The study tested a **package**, not whether each component was necessary. Cross-country heterogeneity and suitability for other settings remain unanswered. Risk-of-bias and updated replications not reviewed here.

## Cross-domain methodological implications

1. **Do not choose a winning intervention from this small selection.** Each study involves different populations, outcomes and institutional conditions.
2. Register at least one adverse, null or economically unfavourable result in every evidence synthesis.
3. Distinguish application from receipt, projected from realised household savings, and programme participation from enduring income/consumption change.
4. For any prospective pilot, define a credible counterfactual, primary outcome, follow-up, total cost, equity/harms and independent methods review.
5. Future review must examine publication bias, external validity, conflicts of interest and contrary findings.
